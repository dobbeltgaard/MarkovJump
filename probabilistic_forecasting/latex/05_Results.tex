\section{Results and discussion} \label{sec:results}
We evaluate the predictive accuracy of the Markov jump process (MJP) under the model settings from \Cref{sec:mjp}, compare against the reference predictors in \Cref{sec:reference_forecasts}, and analyze differences in both prediction error and distributional behavior. In addition to error scores, we assess calibration and discrimination using reliability diagrams and checkerboard plots.

\subsection{Prediction errors} \label{sec:prediction_error}
The prediction errors are calculated using a $k$-fold cross-validation procedure, described in the following. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Choose a $k$. The larger $k$ is chosen, the higher the computational burden becomes \citep{fushiki2011a}. We use $k = 5$ in this study.
    \item By random, divide $\mathcal{S}$ into $k$ subsets of approximately equal size. To ensure reproducibility, a seed for the random number generator should be chosen.
    \item For each of the $k$ subsets (folds):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Initialize $i = 1$. By $\mathcal{S}_i$, we denote the defects belonging to the $i$'th fold. The set of remaining defects is denoted $\mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$, such that $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{S}_i \cup \mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$ and $\mathcal{S}_i \cap \mathcal{S}_{\neg i} = \emptyset$.
        \item Model estimations are performed on the set $\mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$. The parameters of the Markov jump process are determined as described in \Cref{sec:optimum_score_estimation}, and the parameters of the cumulative link model are determined as described in \Cref{sec:cumulative_link_model}.
        \item The models are used to produce predictions of the distributions of $\bm{\pi}^{(s)}\left( t_2^{(s)}\right)$ for the defects $s \in \mathcal{S}_i$ provided their corresponding initial state $y_1^{(s)}$, their initial time stamp $t_1^{(s)}$, and the covariates $\bm{z}^{(s)}$ (for the Markov jump process) or $\bm{z}'^{(s)}$ (for the cumulative link model).
        \item If $i < k$, then increase $i = i + 1$ and GOTO (a); else GOTO 4.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item For each model with the corresponding set of predictions, compute the average prediction errors given all end point observations $y_2^{(s)}$ for $s \in \mathcal{S}$ for each metric described in \Cref{sec:error_metrics}.
\end{enumerate}

We assess the predictive performance of each generator parameterization and assess how exogenous rail characteristics and temporal warping affect prediction accuracy. We use the link function, $g(x) = \exp(x)$, for \cref{eq:trans_rates_covariates}. 
As outlined in \Cref{sec:cumulative_link_model}, we benchmark against cumulative link models (CLMs) with logistic, probit, and complementary log-log link functions. As with the MJP, we also investigate the effect of including exogenous rail characteristics.
The results from the described procedure are presented in \Cref{tab:forecast_error_tab}. An ensemble forecast is constructed by averaging all predictive distributions from all other predictors in the table.

Among the CLMs, the logistic link achieves the best scores, while the complementary log-log link performs worst. Although the logistic assumption underlies the latent variable, this should not be interpreted as evidence that defect propagation itself follows a logistic distribution—only that it provides the most effective link function in this context.

CLMs provide strong static baselines, but MJPs consistently outperform them in log score, reflecting their advantage in capturing temporal dynamics and state dependence. For RPS, the relative difference is smaller: while MJPs are favored in most cases (and especially for the more complex generators), parameterizations $\bm{A}{\RN{1}}$ and $\bm{A}{\RN{2}}$ without warping slightly underperform compared to the logistic CLM.
This pattern is expected: the log score is particularly sensitive to forecast sharpness and the ability to assign high probability to the realized outcome, while the RPS also rewards calibration across the ordinal scale. 
This reflects the trade-off between sharpness and calibration: the generalized Erlang MJP exhibits stronger discrimination but weaker calibration compared to the logistic CLM.
%Consequently, the MJP with the generalized Erlang parameterization demonstrates stronger discrimination ability but weaker calibration relative to the CLM with logistic. %A similar trade-off is observed between the ensemble forecast and the free upper parameterization $\bm{A}'''^{(s)}$.
However, in almost all cases the two different error scores show the same tendencies in the prediction accuracy of the various models, making it more difficult to comment on which models are more calibrated or have better discrimination ability. We therefore provide a detailed diagnostic analysis in \Cref{sec:reliability_analysis}.

The choice of generator parameterization substantially impacts prediction accuracy. 
Overall, the $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ and $\bm{A}_{\RN{5}}$ parameterizations yield the best scores, which indicates that greater model flexibility improves forecast performance for this prediction challenge.
We further compare and discuss the transient distributions implied by each parameterization in \Cref{sec:results_transient_dist_markov}.

Temporal warping (see \cref{sec:warping}) consistently improves prediction accuracy across all parameterizations, indicating that state-dependent rescaling of time can correct for biases introduced by severity classification schemes that do not necessarily align with underlying physical crack sizes. 

Remark: The $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ parameterization is not included under the warped MJP, as estimation did not converge. This could be due to the interaction between the harmonic skip of $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ and the temporal warping weights, creating identifiability issues.

Including exogenous track characteristics likewise reduces prediction error across all investigated model settings of both the MJP and CLM. This is consistent with \citet{bisgaard2024A}, who found that such covariates explain structural variability between rail lines, and supports the notion that the rail network is heterogeneous and that local conditions at the crack location must be accounted for.

The ensemble forecast achieves strong performance, consistent with prior findings that model averaging improves predictive accuracy \citep{wu2021a}. The structural differences between predictors allow them to capture complementary aspects of the data. By pooling their predictions, ensemble averaging reduces idiosyncratic model errors and improves calibration.

\subsection{Transient distributions of the Markov chains} \label{sec:results_transient_dist_markov}
The generator parameterizations imply substantially different degradation dynamics.
In \Cref{fig:transient_dists} (page \pageref{fig:transient_dists}), we illustrate the transient distributions over an eight-year period for a randomly chosen class $3$ defect under each parameterization, along with the corresponding eight-year transition probability matrices.

The generator parameterizations imply substantially different degradation dynamics.
In \Cref{fig:transient_dists} (page \pageref{fig:transient_dists}), we illustrate the transient distributions over an eight-year period for a randomly chosen class $3$ defect under each parameterization, along with the corresponding eight-year transition probability matrices.

The first row of each transition matrix corresponds to the transient distribution after eight years.
Because the generalized Erlang parameterization only allows jumps to neighboring classes, it assigns substantially more probability mass to class $2B$ (see \Cref{fig:trans_dist_gerlang}) than the free upper parameterization (\Cref{fig:trans_dist_free_upper}), which permits direct jumps to more severe categories without passing through intermediate states.
This difference likely reflects the relatively low number of observed class $2B$ defects, causing the free upper model to assign little probability to that state over time.

The reparameterized upper (\Cref{fig:trans_dist_param_upper}) assigns less probability mass to class $2B$ than the generalized Erlang, but more than the free upper. However, it differs notably from both in its treatment of the most severe classes: it assigns substantially more probability mass to class $1$ and correspondingly less to class $0$ after eight years.
This shift stems from the core assumption of the reparameterized upper (\cref{eq:trans_rate_param}), where the degradation time between two observed states is modeled as the sum of intermediate sub-degradation processes.
As a result, probability mass is systematically shifted toward intermediate states, leading to the observed leftward bias in the transition matrix compared to the generalized Erlang and free upper parameterizations.

Although the free upper parameterization achieves the highest predictive accuracy in this study, the reparameterized upper has practical advantages.
The free upper parameterization's number of parameters grows quadratically with the number of states, whereas for the reparameterized upper, it grows only linearly.
For the relatively small state space considered here ($m = 5$), model estimation remains robust. However, for larger state spaces, the free upper may suffer from overparameterization and instability. In such cases, the reparameterized upper offers a alternative, combining linear parameter growth with greater flexibility than the generalized Erlang.

The structural differences between the generator parameterizations reflect different assumptions about the physical degradation mechanisms of rail defects.
While the generalized Erlang parameterization enforces gradual transitions between neighboring severity classes, it can still capture sudden defect propagation through rapid sequences of small jumps.
Conversely, the free upper parameterization allows direct jumps to more severe classes without passing through intermediates, making it well-suited for modeling abrupt failures caused by random shocks, such as those imposed by heavy rolling stock loads or external impacts.
The reparameterized upper parameterization lies between these two extremes: it models large transitions as the cumulative effect of smaller degradation steps, combining gradual wear processes with the possibility of accelerated defect growth.

Thus, all three parameterizations accommodate both gradual and sudden degradation mechanisms, but differ in how flexibly they allow defects to bypass intermediate severity classes.
In practice, real-world rail degradation involves a combination of slow fatigue-driven crack growth and occasional abrupt worsening due to shocks imposed by rolling stock, suggesting that more flexible parameterizations may better approximate the true defect evolution — particularly in long-term forecasts over extended time horizons.

\subsection{Visual forecast diagnostic} \label{sec:reliability_analysis}
Until now, we have evaluated the Markov jump process (MJP) forecasts using proper scoring rules, demonstrating that the MJP with the free upper parameterization ($\bm{A}'''^{(s)}$ from \cref{eq:generator_free_upper}) outperforms all reference predictors.
Including exogenous covariates further improves predictive performance, and combining the MJP with the best-performing cumulative link model in an ensemble forecast yields additional gains.
These results indicate the potential for applying such models to support track maintenance decisions by predicting future defect severity propagation.
However, proper scoring rules such as the RPS, log score, and Brier score, while instructive, summarize forecast quality into single numbers and cannot reveal all sources of forecast deficiencies \citep{hamill1997,Dimitriadis_2024}.

To provide a more detailed analysis, we generate multicategorical reliability diagrams to assess forecast calibration, and checkerboard plots to evaluate discrimination ability.
We follow the methodology proposed by \citet{hamill1997}, extending the framework of reliability diagrams initially introduced for binary forecasts by \citet{wilks1995a}.
The diagnostic plots are generated by the following procedure.
\begin{enumerate}    
    \item Define a set of quantiles $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_K$ (e.g., $0.05, 0.15, \dots, 0.95$), where $K$ is the number of quantiles. We use $K = 10$.
    \item For each forecast, compute the cumulative probabilities across the $m$ categories. For each quantile $q_k$, determine the forecasted category as the smallest category where the cumulative probability exceeds or equals $q_k$. This converts the probabilistic forecasts into categories at each quantile.
    \item For each forecast-observation pair, the multicategory reliability is defined as:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $1$ if the observed category is less than the forecasted category.
        \item $0$ if the observed category is greater than the forecasted category.
        \item $\frac{q - q_{\min}}{q_{\max} - q_{\min}}$ if the observed category equals the forecasted category, where $q_{\min}$ and $q_{\max}$ are the quantiles where the observation matches the forecast.
    \end{itemize}
    \item For each quantile $q_k$, compute the average reliability across all forecast-observation pairs.
\end{enumerate}
Error bars are calculated via a bootstrap procedure, which repeats the above steps across multiple resampled datasets. A checkerboard plot of the difference between the forecasted and observed categories at each quantile is used to visualize the discrimination ability of the forecasts. \citet{hamill1997} offers an exhaustive description of the above methodology. Our GitHub repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/dobbeltgaard/MarkovJump.git}} provides \texttt{R} code to generate these diagnostic plots.

For brevity, we evaluate four well-performing predictors from \cref{tab:forecast_error_tab}:
(1) the uniform distribution predictor (naïve, nonparametric),
(2) the cumulative link model with logistic link and exogenous information,
(3) the Markov jump process with the free upper parameterization and exogenous information, and
(4) the ensemble forecast combining predictors (2) and (3).
Results are shown in \Cref{fig:reliability_diagrams}. Bootstrapping was performed with 200 resamples, and 10\% and 90\% confidence intervals are reported.
\input{figures/reliability_figs}

The empirical distribution predictor (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_empirical_dist_corr}) substantially overestimates the fraction of observed categories across all quantiles, indicating pronounced miscalibration.
Its mean category error is also the highest among the four predictors, consistent with its poor performance on aggregate error scores.

The cumulative link model with logistic link (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_olr_cov}) shows notable improvement in calibration relative to the empirical predictor.
However, its discrimination ability remains modest, as indicated by large average category errors and checkerboard plots with heavily populated bins far from the zero-error line.

The Markov jump process (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_free_upper_tri_TRUE_all}) achieves the lowest mean category error, and its checkerboard plot shows concentration of errors near the zero-category line, indicating good discrimination ability.
Its reliability curve closely follows the ideal diagonal, suggesting well-calibrated forecasts.

The ensemble forecast (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_ensemble}) yields slight improvements in calibration at higher quantiles but slight worsening at lower quantiles.
This behavior can be explained by the interaction of the individual models: while the cumulative link model tends to overpredict severity at higher quantiles, the MJP tends to underpredict, resulting in partial cancellation. However, at lower quantiles, both models tend to underpredict, leading to no corrective effect.

The ensemble forecast appears somewhat less sharp than the MJP alone, as reflected in higher mean category error and more dispersed errors in the checkerboard plots.
Nevertheless, the differences remain relatively minor, and in all cases, the diagnostic plots confirm the improvements in predictive performance indicated by the aggregate scores.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}
We propose modeling partially observed rail defect severity trajectories using a Markov jump process, with transition rates regressed on exogenous information, including tonnage, line speed, rail curvature, grade, and rail profile at the defect locations.
The infinitesimal generator matrix is parameterized in three different ways: a generalized Erlang structure, a reparameterized upper matrix derived from the generalized Erlang form, and a free upper triangular matrix with no functional relationship between transition rates.
Model parameters are estimated through optimum score estimation, maximizing a combined objective based on the average log score, Brier score, and ranked probability score.

We evaluate the predictive performance of the proposed Markov jump processes against several reference predictors, including three naïve predictors (a uniform predictor and an empirical distribution predictor) and a static regression model based on the cumulative link framework, tested with logistic, probit, and complementary log-log link functions.
Prediction errors are estimated using a cross-validation procedure.

The results show that the Markov jump process consistently outperforms all reference predictors, achieving higher predictive accuracy across all error metrics.
Its forecasts are both better calibrated and demonstrate superior discrimination ability.
The parameterization of the generator substantially impacts predictive performance: predictive accuracy increases with the flexibility of the parameterization, allowing larger and more direct jumps in the state space.
Additionally, combining forecasts into an ensemble further improves performance, and including covariate information significantly enhances predictive accuracy.

The proposed methodology provides a competitive forecasting framework for infrastructure managers, offering a practical tool to anticipate the propagation of rail defect severity and thereby support track maintenance decisions.
Beyond railway applications, the approach is also relevant for multicategorical forecasting problems in other domains where ordinal degradation or transition processes must be predicted.
