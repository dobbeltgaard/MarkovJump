\section{Results and discussion} \label{sec:results}
We evaluate the predictive accuracy of the Markov jump process (MJP) under the model settings from \Cref{sec:mjp}, compare against the reference predictors in \Cref{sec:reference_forecasts}, and analyze differences in both prediction error and distributional behavior. In addition to error scores, we assess calibration and discrimination using reliability diagrams and checkerboard plots.

\subsection{Prediction errors} \label{sec:prediction_error}
The prediction errors are calculated using a $k$-fold cross-validation procedure, described in the following. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Choose a $k$. The larger $k$ is chosen, the higher the computational burden becomes \citep{fushiki2011a}. We use $k = 5$ in this study.
    \item By random, divide $\mathcal{S}$ into $k$ subsets of approximately equal size. To ensure reproducibility, a seed for the random number generator should be chosen.
    \item For each of the $k$ subsets (folds):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Initialize $i = 1$. By $\mathcal{S}_i$, we denote the defects belonging to the $i$'th fold. The set of remaining defects is denoted $\mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$, such that $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{S}_i \cup \mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$ and $\mathcal{S}_i \cap \mathcal{S}_{\neg i} = \emptyset$.
        \item Model estimations are performed on the set $\mathcal{S}_{\neg i}$. The parameters of the Markov jump process are determined as described in \Cref{sec:optimum_score_estimation}, and the parameters of the cumulative link model are determined as described in \Cref{sec:cumulative_link_model}.
        \item The models are used to produce predictions of the distributions of $\bm{\pi}^{(s)}\left( t_2^{(s)}\right)$ for the defects $s \in \mathcal{S}_i$ provided their corresponding initial state $y_1^{(s)}$, their initial time stamp $t_1^{(s)}$, and the covariates $\bm{z}^{(s)}$ (for the Markov jump process) or $\bm{z}'^{(s)}$ (for the cumulative link model).
        \item If $i < k$, then increase $i = i + 1$ and GOTO (a); else GOTO 4.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item For each model with the corresponding set of predictions, compute the average prediction errors given all end point observations $y_2^{(s)}$ for $s \in \mathcal{S}$ for each metric described in \Cref{sec:error_metrics}.
\end{enumerate}

We assess the predictive performance of each generator parameterization and assess how exogenous rail characteristics and the state-dependent time scaling affect prediction accuracy. We use the link function, $g(x) = \exp(x)$, for \cref{eq:trans_rates_covariates}. 
As outlined in \Cref{sec:cumulative_link_model}, we benchmark against cumulative link models (CLMs) with logistic, probit, and complementary log-log link functions. As with the MJP, we also investigate the effect of including exogenous rail characteristics.
The results from the described procedure are presented in \Cref{tab:forecast_error_tab} (page \pageref{tab:forecast_error_tab}). An ensemble forecast is constructed by averaging all predictive distributions from all other predictors in the table.

Among the CLMs, the logistic link achieves the best scores, while the complementary log-log link performs worst. Although the logistic assumption underlies the latent variable, this should not be interpreted as evidence that defect propagation itself follows a logistic distribution—only that it provides the most effective link function in this context.

CLMs provide strong static baselines, but MJPs consistently outperform them in log score, reflecting the advantage in capturing temporal dynamics and state dependence. For RPS, the relative difference is smaller: while MJPs are favored in most cases (and especially for the more complex generators), parameterizations $\bm{A}{\RN{1}}$ and $\bm{A}{\RN{2}}$ without the state-dependent time scaling slightly underperform compared to the logistic CLM.
This pattern is expected: the log score is particularly sensitive to forecast sharpness and the ability to assign high probability to the realized outcome, while the RPS also rewards calibration across the ordinal scale. 
This reflects the trade-off between sharpness and calibration: the generalized Erlang MJP exhibits stronger discrimination but weaker calibration compared to the logistic CLM.
%Consequently, the MJP with the generalized Erlang parameterization demonstrates stronger discrimination ability but weaker calibration relative to the CLM with logistic. %A similar trade-off is observed between the ensemble forecast and the free upper parameterization $\bm{A}'''^{(s)}$.
However, in almost all cases the two different error scores show the same tendencies in the prediction accuracy of the various models, making it more difficult to comment on which models are more calibrated or have better discrimination ability. We therefore provide a detailed diagnostic analysis in \Cref{sec:reliability_analysis}.

The choice of generator parameterization substantially impacts prediction accuracy. 
Overall, the $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ and $\bm{A}_{\RN{5}}$ parameterizations yield the best scores, which indicates that greater model flexibility improves forecast performance for this prediction challenge.
In \Cref{sec:results_transient_dist_markov}, we further compare and discuss the transient distributions from chosen examples on parameterizations.

The state-dependent time scaling (see \cref{sec:warping}) consistently improves prediction accuracy across all parameterizations, indicating that state-dependent rescaling of time can correct for biases introduced by severity classification schemes that do not necessarily align with underlying physical crack sizes. 

Remark: The $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ parameterization is not included under the time scaled MJP, as estimation did not converge. This could be due to the interaction between the harmonic skip of $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ and the weights of the state-dependent time scaling, creating identifiability issues.

Including exogenous track characteristics likewise reduces prediction error across all investigated model settings of both the MJP and CLM. This is consistent with \citet{bisgaard2024A}, who found that such covariates explain structural variability between rail lines, and supports the notion that the rail network is heterogeneous and that local conditions at the crack location must be accounted for.

The ensemble forecast achieves strong performance, consistent with prior findings that model averaging improves predictive accuracy \citep{wu2021a}. The structural differences between predictors allow them to capture complementary aspects of the data. By pooling their predictions, ensemble averaging reduces idiosyncratic model errors and improves calibration.

\subsection{Transient distributions of the Markov chains} \label{sec:results_transient_dist_markov}
The generator parameterizations imply substantially different degradation dynamics.
In \Cref{fig:transient_dists}, %(page \pageref{fig:transient_dists}), 
we illustrate the transient distributions over an eight-year period for a randomly chosen class $3$ defect
under the parameterization of the generalized Erlang ($\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$), the relaxed Erlang ($\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$), and the upper-banded (max jump = 3) ($\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$), 
along with the corresponding eight-year transition probability matrices.


The first row of each transition matrix corresponds to the transient distribution after eight years.
Because $\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$ only allows jumps to neighboring classes, it assigns substantially more probability mass to class $2B$ (see \Cref{fig:trans_dist_gerlang}) than $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ (\Cref{fig:trans_dist_tridiagonal}), which permits direct jumps to more severe categories without passing through all intermediate states.
This difference likely reflects the relatively low number of observed class $2B$ defects, causing $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ to assign little probability to that state over time.

$\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ (\Cref{fig:trans_dist_gerlang_relax}) assigns less probability mass to class $2B$ than $\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$, but more than $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$. However, it differs notably from both in its treatment of the most severe classes: it assigns substantially more probability mass to class $1$ and correspondingly less to class $0$ after eight years.
This shift stems from the core assumption of $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ (\cref{eq:trans_rate_param}), where the degradation time between two observed states is modeled as the sum of intermediate sub-degradation processes.
As a result, probability mass is systematically shifted toward intermediate states, leading to the observed leftward bias in the transition matrix compared to $\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$ and $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$.

The structural differences between the generator parameterizations reflect different assumptions about the physical degradation mechanisms of rail defects.
%While $\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$ enforces gradual transitions between neighboring severity classes, it can still capture sudden defect propagation through rapid sequences of small jumps.Conversely, $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ (as well as $\bm{A}_{\RN{3}}$ and $\bm{A}_{\RN{5}}$) allows direct jumps to more severe classes without passing through intermediates, making it well-suited for modeling abrupt failures caused by random shocks, such as those imposed by heavy rolling stock loads or external impacts. $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ lies between these two extremes: it models large transitions as the cumulative effect of smaller degradation steps, combining gradual wear processes with the possibility of accelerated defect growth.
In summary, $\bm{A}_{\RN{1}}$ enforces gradual progression via neighboring classes, $\bm{A}_{\RN{4}}$ (and similarly $\bm{A}_{\RN{3}}$ and $\bm{A}_{\RN{5}}$) allows for abrupt jumps to severe states, and $\bm{A}_{\RN{2}}$ represents an intermediate compromise. Real-world degradation involves both fatigue-driven crack growth and occasional abrupt worsening due to shocks imposed by rolling stock, suggesting that the more flexible parameterizations better approximate true defect evolution, particularly in long-term forecasts over extended time horizons.

As the generalized Erlang only models jumps to the neighboring class, it assigns substantially more probability mass to class $2B$ (see \Cref{fig:trans_dist_gerlang}) than the free upper parameterization in \Cref{fig:trans_dist_free_upper}, which is not restricted to visit $2B$ before it can move further into more severe categories. \subsection{Forecast diagnostics of reliability and discrimination} \label{sec:reliability_analysis}
So far, forecast evaluation has relied on proper scoring rules. These showed that flexible MJP parameterizations outperform reference predictors, and that state-dependent time scaling, exogenous information, and averaging over all predictors in an ensemble further improve accuracy. However, such scores reduce forecast quality to single summary values, and therefore cannot reveal the specific sources of deficiencies \citep{hamill1997,Dimitriadis_2024}.

To provide a more detailed analysis, we generate multicategorical reliability diagrams to assess forecast calibration, and checkerboard plots to evaluate discrimination ability.
We follow the methodology proposed by \citet{hamill1997}, extending the framework of reliability diagrams initially introduced for binary forecasts by \citet{wilks1995a}.
The diagnostic plots are generated by the following procedure.
\begin{enumerate}    
    \item Define a set of quantiles $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_K$ (e.g., $0.05, 0.15, \dots, 0.95$), where $K$ is the number of quantiles. We use $K = 10$.
    \item For each forecast, compute the cumulative probabilities across the $m$ categories. For each quantile $q_k$, determine the forecasted category as the smallest category where the cumulative probability exceeds or equals $q_k$. This converts the probabilistic forecasts into categories at each quantile.
    \item For each forecast-observation pair, the multicategory reliability is defined as:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $1$ if the observed category is less than the forecasted category.
        \item $0$ if the observed category is greater than the forecasted category.
        \item $\frac{q - q_{\min}}{q_{\max} - q_{\min}}$ if the observed category equals the forecasted category, where $q_{\min}$ and $q_{\max}$ are the quantiles where the observation matches the forecast.
    \end{itemize}
    \item For each quantile $q_k$, compute the average reliability across all forecast-observation pairs.
\end{enumerate}
As a supplementary summary metric, we report the \textit{average category error}, defined as the mean absolute difference between the forecasted category at each quantile and the observed category, averaged first across forecasts and then across quantiles.

Error bars are calculated via a bootstrap procedure, which repeats the above steps across multiple resampled datasets. A checkerboard plot of the difference between the forecasted and observed categories at each quantile is used to visualize the discrimination ability of the forecasts. \citet{hamill1997} offers an exhaustive description of the above methodology. Our GitHub repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/dobbeltgaard/MarkovJump.git}} provides \texttt{R} code to generate these diagnostic plots.

The uniform distribution predictor (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_uniform}) substantially overestimates the fraction of observed categories across all quantiles, indicating pronounced miscalibration.
Its average category error is also the highest among the four predictors, consistent with its poor performance on aggregate error scores in \cref{tab:forecast_error_tab}.

The cumulative link model with logistic link (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_olr_cov}) shows notable improvement in calibration relative to the uniform distribution predictor.
However, its discrimination ability remains modest, as indicated by large average category errors and checkerboard plots with heavily populated bins far from the zero-error line.

The Markov jump process (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_bidiagonal_TRUE_exp_exp_all_warp}) achieves the lowest mean category error among the single predictors, and its checkerboard plot shows concentration of errors near the zero-category line, indicating good discrimination ability.
Its reliability curve closely follows the ideal diagonal, suggesting well-calibrated forecasts.

The ensemble forecast (\Cref{fig:reliability_diag_ensemble_all}) yields slight improvements in calibration at higher quantiles but slight worsening at lower quantiles.
This behavior can be explained by the interaction of the individual models: while the cumulative link model tends to overpredict severity at higher quantiles, the MJP tends to underpredict, resulting in partial cancellation. However, at lower quantiles, both models tend to underpredict, leading to no corrective effect.

The ensemble forecast appears somewhat less sharp than the MJP alone, as reflected in higher mean category error and more dispersed errors in the checkerboard plots.
Nevertheless, the differences remain relatively minor, and in all cases, the diagnostic plots confirm the improvements in predictive performance indicated by the aggregate scores.

\subsection{Proposition for use case} \label{sec:proposition_for_use}
From the perspective of an infrastructure manager, probabilistic forecasts in isolation have limited operational value. 
To turn them into actionable strategies, forecasts must be embedded in decision frameworks that allocate inspection and maintenance resources under uncertainty.

Discussions with BaneNOR and Banedanmark (the infrastructure managers responsible for most of the Norwegian and Danish networks) highlighted that connecting predictions to actionable maintenance strategies is essential for realizing the value of predictive models. In practice, this requires translating forecast distributions of defect severity into decisions that balance safety, cost, and resource constraints. 

One possible implementation pathway is a threshold-based allocation under a fixed budget: for each defect $s\in\mathcal{S}$, generate forecasts over a six-month horizon and allocate resources to the $n$ defects with the highest probability of reaching a critical “rail-break” state, with $n$ bounded by the budget divided by the average per-defect action cost.

Alternatively, the predictive distributions can be embedded into a stochastic optimization problem. 
Since we consider a discrete stochastic process, it can be represented by a finite set of scenarios over a time partition $\widehat{T}=(T_1,\dots,T_n)$, where $T_n$ is the decision horizon.
Given active defects $\mathcal{S}$ and a rail line mapping $\ell:\mathcal{S}\to\{\text{rail lines}\}$, let $\widehat{\bm\pi}^{(s)}_j(t)=\mathds{P}(X^{(s)}(t)=j)$ be the predicted probability for class $j\in\mathcal{J}$ at time $t\in\widehat{T}$ (cf. \cref{eq:transient_dist}). Define
\[
y^{(s)}(t)\in\{0,1\}\ \ \text{(inspection at $t$)},\qquad
z^{(s)}(t)\in\{0,1\}\ \ \text{(maintenance at $t$)}.
\]
Let $q^{(s)}_{\mathrm{break}}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{crit}}}\widehat{\bm\pi}^{(s)}_j(t)$ be the predicted probability of being in a critical class set $\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{crit}}$ by $t$.
Let $c_{\mathrm{ins}}^{\ell}$ be rail line specific inspection cost, $c_{\mathrm{maint}}^{\ell}(j)$ the (severity-dependent) maintenance cost, and $c_{\mathrm{fail}}^{\ell}$ the monetized expected loss of a rail break on line $\ell$ (e.g. combining derailment risk and societal cost).
Let $B(t)$ denote the per-period budget (or a total budget $B$).
The objective (considering risk, repairs, and inspection) can then be written as the expected cost model
\begin{align}
\min_{y,z}\ \ 
&\sum_{t\in\widehat{T}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
\Big[
c_{\mathrm{ins}}^{\ell(s)}\,y^{(s)}(t)
+ z^{(s)}(t)\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} c_{\mathrm{maint}}^{\ell(s)}(j)\,\widehat{\bm\pi}^{(s)}_{j}(t)
\Big]
+ \sum_{t\in\widehat{T}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} 
c_{\mathrm{fail}}^{\ell(s)}\, q^{(s)}_{\mathrm{break}}(t)\,\Bigl(1-\!\!\sum_{\tau\le t} z^{(s)}(\tau)\Bigr)
\label{eq:obj-expected}\\[-0.25em]
\text{s.t.}\ \ 
&\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\!\Big(c_{\mathrm{ins}}^{\ell(s)}\,y^{(s)}(t) 
+ z^{(s)}(t)\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} c_{\mathrm{maint}}^{\ell(s)}(j)\,\widehat{\bm\pi}^{(s)}_{j}(t)\Big)\ \le\ B(t), \quad \forall t\in\widehat{T}, \label{eq:budget}\\
&y^{(s)}(t),z^{(s)}(t)\in\{0,1\}, \qquad
y^{(s)}(t)+z^{(s)}(t)\le 1, \quad \forall s\in\mathcal{S},\ \forall t\in\widehat{T}. \label{eq:binary}
\end{align}
The factor $\bigl(1-\sum_{\tau\le t} z^{(s)}(\tau)\bigr)$ switches off break losses once maintenance is performed (assuming maintenance returns the defect to a safe state). The constraints enforce the budget and at most one action per defect and period.
Extensions can model information gains from inspection (scenario updates), crew logistics, or multi-horizon planning.
Beyond the expected-cost objective, there are many variants, e.g. robust programs minimizing with respect to worst case scenarios.

The focus of this study has been to develop probabilistic models for forecasting defect severity and evaluate their accuracy. 
The decision-making program sketched here is intended as a proposal for how such forecasts could be used in infrastructure manager operations, rather than as a prescriptive solution.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}
We have proposed modeling partially observed rail defect severity trajectories using a Markov jump process (MJP) estimated via optimum score estimation, aligning training with the chosen forecast evaluation metric. The framework accommodates covariate-dependent transition rates (tonnage, line speed, curvature, grade, rail profile), multiple generator parameterizations, and a scaling of the effective process clock across severity classes.

Through systematic forecast evaluation with proper scoring rules, we benchmarked MJPs against reference predictors, including a uniform predictor and cumulative link models with logistic, probit, and complementary log-log links. Results from cross-validation show that MJPs consistently outperform all reference predictors, achieving higher accuracy across both log score and ranked probability score. Forecast diagnostics confirm that MJPs provide better calibration and discrimination ability, while ensemble forecasts further enhance accuracy.

The choice of generator parameterization strongly affects predictive performance: more flexible structures, which allow larger or skip transitions, yield higher accuracy. State-dependent time scaling improves performance across all parameterizations, and including exogenous information further enhances predictive accuracy, reflecting the heterogeneity of local track conditions.

Finally, we sketched how predictive distributions can be embedded in a stochastic optimization program to allocate inspection and maintenance actions under budget constraints. This illustrates how probabilistic forecasts can be connected to actionable decision-making for infrastructure managers, bridging the gap between statistical modeling and practical maintenance planning.

The proposed methodology thus contributes both theoretically and practically: it advances CTMC-based degradation modeling by introducing state-dependent time scaling and scoring-rule-consistent estimation, and it demonstrates how probabilistic forecasts can support infrastructure management. Beyond railways, the framework is broadly applicable to multicategorical forecasting problems in other domains where degradation processes evolve over ordinal state spaces and inspection intervals are irregular.
